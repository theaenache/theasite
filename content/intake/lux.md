WOWWWW.

I cried at least 3 times at her tour when she performed this live (no backtrack, with a LIVE ORCHESTRA btw). She is the most incredible vocalist I have witnessed in my life.

When I first heard the single for this album, Berghain, I was taken aback. I felt like I was being dragged through a wind tunnel at 250 km/h into a blinding light. As a longtime Rosalía enjoyer, I am never shocked when she pivots into a new vibe or energy during whatever project she embarks on. I honestly expect it. However, the density and versatility of this album were something different that I have never really experienced from anyone before.

First, as a polyglot (and more importantly, in this context, someone interested in global language and culture), when I heard that this album was featuring FOURTEEN DIFFERENT LANGUAGES (??!!), I was floored. Adding on to the language complexity, the album is structured as a journey from loving the world to loving God, back to the earth. No matter what your religion or your spirituality looks like, you can self-insert into this connection to the world as someone existing, loving, and moving through it. The language and the stylistic choices for the narrative are extremely intentional. Most tracks clearly map to a specific religious figure whose story somehow perfectly aligns with the album.

After I decided to open my ears and brain instead of just vibing to the incredible lyrical and musical range of this album, I came to some realizations. First, that the language choice of the song often matched the language of the cultural background or context of the figure inspiring the track. For instance, due to the nature/vibe of the Berghain track, when I first listened, I was under the impression that she was just paying homage to the club in Berlin. Well, she certainly is to an extent. Lucky for me, I had 10 whole days to listen to Berghain over and over and over again until the rest of the album dropped, so I had some significant context on this song when we got the rest of the album. What is actually happening here is a sonic and lyrical bridge between medieval religious ecstasy and modern underground rave culture. The historical figure for this track is the 12th-century German polymath and mystic Saint Hildegard von Bingen. The intro to the song is a translation of one of Hildegard's famous vision descriptions:

"Heaven was opened and a fiery light of exceeding brilliance came and permeated my whole brain and inflamed my whole heart."

This becomes:

"Die Flamme dringt in mein Gehirn ein / Wie ein Blei-Teddybär"

In other words, "The flame penetrates my brain / like a lead teddy bear."

To tie the loose strings together, Hildegard's vision is divine light flooding the body. This ecstasy is explicitly religious; the subject is self-dissolving upward into God. What our queen Rosalía is doing here is keeping the mechanism driving this ecstasy (the flame, the permeation of the brain, the heart being made unbearably heavy), and then stripping the certainty of its object. What is left here is being overwhelmed and undone, which happens to be the exact vibe of a rave (the untz untz shaking your bones, allowing yourself to "get lost" in it, and the chemical dissolution of the ego at 6 a.m.): physiologically, it's the same event Hildegard was straining to describe, just relocated from the cloister to Berghain. When she performs this live, it is performed as a techno remix and a techno club moment with all of her backup dancers after she hits the most diabolical opera notes — no autotune — for the first few minutes of the song.

Another theme on this album that I didn't really clock until my 2nd or 3rd listen is how much of LUX is defined by women walking away from men, marriage, and male-defined worth in the name of something greater. I think we can all see that in "La Perla", but it's actually woven throughout.

Rosalía broke off her engagement to Rosalía's "La Perla" (ehemRaul ehemAlejandro) in the summer of 2023 while cooking up this masterpiece. That theme of rejection in pursuit of something more runs through the veins of this whole record. She refracts her experience through a whole roster of religious figures who did the same.

Her patron saint, St. Rosalía of Palermo, is the subject of "Focu 'Ranni", sung in Sicilian. She was a 12th-century Norman noblewoman from a family who claimed descent from Charlemagne, and when a count who'd saved the king's life asked for her hand, she not only refused, but cut off her hair, took holy vows, and walked out on the whole arrangement to live and die alone as a hermit in a cave on Monte Pellegrino. The worst she can say is no boys.

On "Porcelana", I was blessed to unearth the incredible story of Ryōnen Gensō, a 17th-century Japanese Zen nun. She was forced into TWO marriages with older men. In these marriages, she had a bunch of kids (likely also not her choice), and then afterwards chose to become a nun. She wanted to participate in monastic life, but a Buddhist master decided her beauty was "too distracting" for the male students. So, she pressed a hot iron to her own face and disfigured it to get in. She scarred her own face rather than allow men to reduce her to her beauty. This act of self-disfigurement teeters into self-deification in this song through Rosalía's overlay and interpretation. This is exemplified as the Latin refrain slides from ego sum nihil, "I am nothing", to ego sum lux mundi, "I am the light of the world", a direct bar from Jesus in John 8:12. You may be thinking, okay... self-deification, you say... Rosalía, you're the goat but you're not God. No worries, audience! She is not claiming to be. Taking a step back, this whole album is framed as a search. Rosalía is extremely religious. She did the Camino de Santiago by herself, prays every night, and described this project as a gift back to God. She is not trying to replace deity here, but she is reaching towards it. She wants to be one with it, not it.

Another even more worldly version of this narrative showed up via Fabiola of Rome. She escaped a young, violent, and adulterous husband, gave her wealth to the poor, and ended up the patron saint of divorcees and abuse survivors. Rosalía has stated that she is the subject of "La Perla". A lot of people are able to draw parallels into Rosalía's life with this song, but in my humble opinion, I think pretty much anyone can self-insert with this song. The title of this track is a word used to describe a real piece of work. She calls the perla at hand an emotional terrorist, the biggest disaster in the world, thief of peace, playboy, minefield for my sensitivity, Olympic gold medal for the biggest jerk (just to name a few). I think some of her other choice words like "su masterpiece, su colección de bras" are Rosalía talking her smack about her ex-fiancé who collected bras that were thrown on stage at him. However, this track isn't exclusively about ehemRaul ehemAlejandro, but rather Rosalía resonating with the saintly subject of this song through her lived experience. Raul is not bigger than the program. He doesn't get special treatment and a dedicated song in this album. We've all dealt with a perla. During her shows, she invites guests to discuss their experiences with a perla before performing this song, ideally getting the audience to consider self-insertion into this narrative. Fabiola's connection is actually exactly what lets this make sense within the context of the general theme of this album, although it reads as a breakup diss on the surface.

Anyway, what really gets me about this theme is that the pursuit of "more" isn't always the same song to song. Sometimes it's God, sometimes it's freedom, sometimes it's just the refusal to let a man be or define the ceiling of your life. Whatever it is, I'm obsessed.

All in all, the album transitions seamlessly from the top to the bottom through extremely significant energy switches, languages, and styles in a way that somehow doesn't give the listener whiplash. She wrote this entire album herself, recorded it with the London Symphony, and intentionally didn't use loops or artificial modification/support in any capacity. In a world so entrenched with artificial intelligence and letting other people do the thinking for you while you just consume others' creations all day, I really needed something like this personally. It made the album all the more special to me. I should be shocked that she was able to pull this off, but she has long been the GOAT. Rosalía was one of the extremely rare admits into Escola Superior de Música de Catalunya in the flamenco interpretation program (they accept literally one person a year).

I could waffle on about my interpretation from song to song for hours, but long story short, this album was perfect and I recommend everyone listen to it top to bottom at least once. It is also a great meditation on spirituality if you are Spanish speaking.
